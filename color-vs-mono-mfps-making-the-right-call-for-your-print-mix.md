# Color vs. Mono MFPs: Making the Right Call for Your Print Mix

Walk into any showroom and the color multifunction printer looks like the obvious choice. It does everything the mono machine does, plus color. Why would anyone deliberately buy the model that can do less? That instinct is exactly how offices end up paying color prices for years to print documents that were black text all along.

The honest answer is that neither machine is better. The right MFP depends entirely on your print mix — the actual balance of color and black-and-white pages your office produces month after month. Get that balance right and the machine pays for itself quietly. Get it wrong and you either overpay on every page or find yourself unable to print the one color proposal that mattered.

This guide explains the real cost difference, how to read your own print mix, and how to make the call with confidence.

---

> **Key Takeaways**
> - Color pages typically cost 5 to 15 times more per page than black-and-white, because a color device uses four toners instead of one.
> - A monochrome MFP is the cheaper, faster choice for offices that mostly print text: invoices, contracts, reports, and internal documents.
> - A color MFP earns its place when client-facing materials, marketing, charts, or branded documents are a regular part of the workload.
> - The smartest setups often mix both machines and add print rules, so color is used deliberately rather than by accident.
> - The decision should be driven by a real count of your color-versus-mono pages, not by the feature list on the box.

## What Actually Separates a Color MFP From a Mono One

A monochrome MFP prints only in black. A color MFP prints in full color and in black. The difference sounds small, but it drives every cost and speed difference that follows, because of how the two machines are built.

A mono device uses a single black toner cartridge. A color device uses four — cyan, magenta, yellow, and black — plus the extra imaging hardware to combine them. That is more parts to buy, more parts to replace, and more toner feeding every page. [1800 Office Solutions' monochrome printing guide](https://1800officesolutions.com/monochrome-printing-guide-2025/) explains the direct consequence: because a mono printer needs only one cartridge instead of four, its cost per page is dramatically lower, and it typically runs faster on text-heavy work.

[Marco's comparison of black-and-white versus color printers](https://www.marconet.com/blog/black-and-white-vs.-color-printers) frames the trade-off cleanly. Mono machines are built for volume text printing: high speed, low running cost, fewer consumables to manage. Color machines are built for versatility: the same everyday printing plus anything that needs to look designed. The question is never which is more capable — it is which capability your office actually uses.

## How Much More Does Color Really Cost?

Color is not slightly more expensive than black-and-white. It is several times more expensive, on every single page. In 2026, industry pricing guides put color prints at roughly 5 to 15 times the per-page cost of mono, depending on the machine and toner ([Ink Toner Store, "Color Ink vs. Black-and-White Printing Cost Analysis"](https://blog.inktonerstore.com/color-ink-vs-black-and-white-printing-cost-analysis/)). A black-and-white laser page is measured in a few fils; a color page with real graphics can cost many times that.

The reason is simple: a color page can draw from all four toners at once, while a mono page uses only black. [EO Johnson's breakdown of the cost of printing in color versus black and white](https://www.eojohnson.com/blog/managed-print-provider/cost-of-printing-in-color-vs-black-and-white) shows how quickly that multiplies at office volumes. An office printing thousands of pages a month in color rather than mono is not spending a little more — it is spending a large, recurring sum that compounds every month for the life of the machine.

Here is the trap that catches most offices. A color MFP does not ask whether a page needs color. Unless you tell it otherwise, it prints a plain text email in color mode, quietly charging you the color rate for a page that has no color on it. That is how offices with color machines end up with color bills far larger than their actual color needs. The machine is not the problem; the default setting is.

## When a Monochrome MFP Is the Right Choice

Choose mono when the overwhelming majority of your printing is text. [WiZiX's guide to whether your office needs a monochrome printer](https://wizixtech.com/does-your-office-need-a-monochrome-printer/) is direct about the profile: businesses that mainly print invoices, contracts, memos, reports, and internal paperwork get faster output and far lower running costs from a dedicated mono device.

Think about what most offices actually print all day. Accounts prints invoices and statements. Legal prints contracts and correspondence. HR prints policies and forms. Operations prints reports and picking lists. None of that needs a single drop of color. For these workloads, a color MFP is not adding value — it is adding cost, because even in black-and-white mode a four-toner machine carries higher consumable and maintenance overhead than a purpose-built mono unit.

Mono devices also tend to be simpler and more durable at high volume. Fewer consumables means fewer things to reorder and fewer things to fail. For a department pushing large monthly volumes of routine paperwork, that reliability is worth as much as the lower per-page price. If your honest answer to "how often do we print color?" is "almost never," the mono machine is not a compromise — it is the correct tool.

## When a Color MFP Earns Its Keep

Choose color when client-facing and marketing output is a genuine, recurring part of your work — not an occasional exception. [DSBLS's analysis of color versus black-and-white printing](https://www.dsbls.com/resources/color-vs-black-and-white-printing/) makes the case that for proposals, presentations, branded documents, and marketing collateral, color is not a luxury; it is what makes the material do its job.

Some documents simply fail in black-and-white. A pie chart where every slice is a shade of grey is unreadable. A branded proposal printed in mono looks like a draft. A product sheet loses its photographs. A pitch deck loses its impact. If your office regularly produces these — a sales team printing proposals, a marketing team running short-run flyers, a consultancy delivering branded reports — a color MFP pays for its higher running cost in the value of the output.

The key word is regularly. If you print color materials most weeks, owning a color MFP is cheaper and faster than the alternative of running to a print shop for every job. But if color is a once-a-quarter event, the economics can flip — and that is where the smartest offices stop choosing between the two machines and start combining them.

## The Best Answer Is Often Both, Plus Rules

For many offices, the right call is not color or mono — it is a fleet that uses each where it fits, governed by print rules. This is the approach that consistently produces the lowest total cost without sacrificing the color output that matters.

The setup is straightforward. A high-volume mono MFP handles the bulk of everyday text printing at the lowest possible cost per page. A color MFP sits alongside it for the client-facing and marketing work. Then print management rules make sure jobs land on the right machine: emails, drafts, and internal documents default to mono, while color is available deliberately when someone actually needs it.

This directly kills the most expensive habit in color printing — accidental color. [Toner Buzz's guide to calculating your printing costs](https://www.tonerbuzz.com/blog/printing-costs/) reinforces the underlying discipline: you cannot control a cost you do not measure, and per-page tracking by color and mono is what tells you whether your mix is drifting. Set grayscale and duplex as defaults, route color to a single managed device, and require color to be a choice rather than an accident. The result is an office that prints color when it counts and pays mono rates the rest of the time.

For most businesses, that combination beats owning only color machines (too expensive per page) or only mono machines (unable to produce the materials clients see).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much more does a color page cost than a black-and-white page?

In 2026, industry pricing guides put color pages at roughly 5 to 15 times the per-page cost of black-and-white, because a color device uses four toners instead of one. Heavy-graphics and photo pages sit at the top of that range. At office volumes, that multiplier turns into a large, recurring difference every month.

### Can a color MFP print in black-and-white to save money?

Yes, and it should be set to do so by default. A color MFP prints grayscale on demand, but unless the driver defaults to mono, it prints everyday documents in color mode and charges the color rate. Even in mono mode, though, a four-toner machine carries higher overhead than a dedicated monochrome device at high volume.

### Is a monochrome printer faster than a color one?

Generally yes for text work. Monochrome laser MFPs are built for high-speed text output, often exceeding 30 pages per minute, and they carry fewer consumables to manage. For offices printing large volumes of invoices, contracts, and reports, that speed and simplicity is a real operational advantage over a color machine.

### We only print color occasionally. Do we still need a color MFP?

Probably not a dedicated one for everyone. If color is a rare event, a single shared color MFP alongside your mono fleet, or occasional outsourcing, is cheaper than putting color machines on every desk. Reserve color capacity for where it is genuinely used, and let mono handle the everyday volume.

## Conclusion

The color-versus-mono decision is not about which machine is better — it is about which one matches your print mix. Offices that mostly print text save substantially with monochrome MFPs. Offices that regularly produce proposals, marketing, and branded documents need color to do that work well. And many offices land on the smartest answer of all: both machines, with print rules that send each job to the right one and make color a deliberate choice.

Before you decide, do the one thing that settles it: pull a month of print data and count your color pages against your mono pages. That single ratio tells you which machine — or which combination — is right, far better than any spec sheet.
