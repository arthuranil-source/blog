# Back to School: MFP Prep for Educational Institutions

The start of a new academic year brings more than new timetables, classrooms, and student registrations. It also brings a sharp increase in document activity. Teachers print lesson materials, administrators prepare forms, finance departments issue documents, and students may need handouts, assessments, and project materials. For this reason, preparing multifunction printers (MFPs) before school reopens is an important part of technology readiness.

An MFP is not simply a printer. It can print, copy, scan, email, store, and sometimes finish documents by stapling, sorting, or punching holes. If these devices are not tested and maintained before the first week of school, even a small problem can create delays across departments.

Educational institutions still depend on MFPs because many school processes continue to require printed, copied, signed, scanned, and securely stored documents. A reliable MFP fleet helps schools keep these workflows moving while controlling costs, protecting information, and reducing unnecessary equipment downtime.

## Why Schools Still Depend on MFPs

Schools are becoming more digital, but paper has not disappeared. Many activities still require physical documents or a combination of paper and digital records.

Schools commonly use MFPs for:

- Classroom worksheets and reading materials.
- Examinations, assessment papers, and revision packs.
- Student registration and admission documents.
- Permission forms and medical records.
- Invoices, purchase orders, and finance paperwork.
- Staff contracts, letters, and internal notices.
- Certificates, event programmes, and meeting documents.
- Scanning paper records into shared folders or document systems.

A school may also need to produce large quantities of documents within a short period. Examination weeks, enrolment periods, parent meetings, and the beginning of a term can create sudden spikes in printing demand.

This is why an MFP that worked adequately during a quiet holiday period may struggle when hundreds of staff members return. A first-week technology checklist should include testing printers and copiers under normal demand, checking supplies, confirming network access, and verifying that scan and secure-print features work correctly.[^1]

The objective is not to purchase the fastest device available. It is to ensure that each MFP is suitable for the volume, document types, users, and security requirements of the school.

## Begin With a Complete MFP Audit

The first step in back-to-school preparation is to create an accurate inventory. Do not rely on old purchasing records alone. Walk through the school and record every MFP, printer, scanner, and shared device.

For each device, document:

- Location and department.
- Device type and main functions.
- Approximate age.
- Black-and-white or colour capability.
- A4, A3, or other supported paper sizes.
- Monthly page volume.
- Current meter reading.
- Service contract details.
- Warranty status.
- Known faults or recurring error messages.
- Required toner, staples, drums, and other consumables.

A proper asset audit should record equipment age, condition, location, warranty information, usage, and service history. This information helps the school identify which machines need attention before reopening and which devices may be approaching replacement.[^2][^3]

The audit should also identify whether some devices are overloaded while others are rarely used. If one MFP is located too far from classrooms or receives most of the school’s print traffic, users may experience queues and delays even when the device itself is functioning correctly.

## Inspect and Clean Each Device

Dust, paper debris, adhesive residue, and small pieces of torn paper can cause feeding problems and poor print quality. During the holiday period, MFPs may also remain unused for several weeks, allowing dust to collect around trays, vents, scanning glass, and output areas.

A basic inspection should include:

- Cleaning the scanner glass and document feeder.
- Removing dust from accessible external areas.
- Checking paper trays for scraps and damaged guides.
- Inspecting output trays and finishing units.
- Looking for toner spills or powder accumulation.
- Checking power cables and network connections.
- Confirming that ventilation areas are not blocked.

Internal cleaning and replacement of worn components should be performed by trained technicians where required. School staff should not dismantle areas containing high-voltage components or attempt repairs beyond their training.

Preventive maintenance is more effective when it is planned rather than performed only after a breakdown. School maintenance guidance recommends combining inspection, scheduled maintenance, written procedures, staff training, and clear responsibility for each task.[^4][^5]

For an MFP fleet, this means scheduling technical servicing during school holidays, weekends, or other low-demand periods. A planned service visit may identify worn feed rollers, failing separation pads, weak finishing mechanisms, or other parts before they cause a complete stoppage.

## Test Printing, Copying, and Scanning

A device should not be considered ready simply because it powers on. Each major function must be tested using the types of documents the school normally produces.

Run tests for:

- Single-sided and double-sided printing.
- Black-and-white and colour output, where applicable.
- A4 and A3 printing, where required.
- Multiple-page documents.
- Documents containing images, tables, and small text.
- Copying through the document feeder.
- Scanning from the glass and feeder.
- Scan-to-email and scan-to-folder.
- Stapling, sorting, folding, or hole punching, if installed.

Testing should include actual classroom and administrative files rather than only a simple test page. A document may print correctly from one computer but fail when sent through a shared queue or when processed by the MFP’s finishing unit.

The school should also confirm that print jobs are not being held, duplicated, or sent to the wrong device. These problems are often caused by incorrect drivers, old print queues, network changes, or user access settings rather than mechanical faults.[^1]

## Check Supplies Before Demand Increases

Running out of toner or paper during the first week of term is avoidable. Schools should check current stock levels and compare them with expected demand.

Essential supplies may include:

- Black and colour toner.
- Staple cartridges.
- Waste toner containers.
- Replacement drums or imaging units, where applicable.
- A range of paper sizes and weights.
- Labels, envelopes, and specialist media.

Do not order supplies based only on the number of devices. Review actual page volumes and the expected activity for the coming term. A school that prints examinations or large learning packs may need more stock before reopening than it uses during an ordinary month.

At the same time, excessive stock creates its own problems. Supplies can become obsolete when equipment is replaced, and poorly stored paper can absorb moisture or become damaged. A managed print service guide for schools recommends remote monitoring of consumables and service requirements to reduce both shortages and unnecessary overstocking.[^6]

Store paper in a dry, clean area and keep packaging sealed until the paper is needed. Damp or curled paper can lead to misfeeds, wrinkled output, and repeated print failures.

## Reconnect Devices to the School Network

Network changes are common during the summer break. Schools may upgrade wireless systems, replace servers, change internet providers, update security settings, or reorganise user accounts. These changes can prevent staff computers from communicating with MFPs.

Before staff return, the IT team should verify:

- The MFP has the correct network connection.
- The device has the correct IP address or hostname.
- Staff computers can locate the correct print queue.
- Approved mobile devices can print where permitted.
- Scan-to-email settings are working.
- Scan-to-folder destinations are accessible.
- Address books and user permissions are current.
- Print drivers are compatible with school computers.
- Firmware and device software are approved and up to date.

Testing should be completed from representative locations, including administration offices, staff rooms, libraries, and classrooms. A device may be reachable from the IT office but unavailable from another building or network segment.

The school should also remove old print queues and clearly label the current devices. Confusing names such as “Printer 4” or “Copy Room Device” can cause users to select the wrong machine and leave documents unattended.

## Protect Student and Staff Information

MFPs process sensitive information. A copied medical form, scanned student record, examination paper, or staff document may contain confidential data. Back-to-school preparation should therefore include a review of security settings.

Important checks include:

- Secure print or PIN release is functioning.
- User authentication is enabled where required.
- Stored jobs and temporary files are managed correctly.
- Scan destinations are limited to authorised users.
- Administrator passwords are not left at default settings.
- Address books contain only approved contacts.
- Unclaimed documents are removed from output trays.
- Staff understand how to collect confidential print jobs.

A secure-print function holds a document until the user enters a PIN or authenticates at the device. This reduces the risk of confidential papers being left in a shared area.

Schools should also review who can use colour printing, large-format paper, scanning, and administrative functions. Access should reflect job responsibilities. Teachers may need routine classroom printing, while finance and administration teams may need secure scanning and document distribution.

Multifactor authentication can provide stronger account protection for educational organisations, particularly when staff access systems containing sensitive information.  MFP security should form part of the wider school technology and information-protection plan rather than being treated as a separate hardware issue.[^7]

## Train Staff Before the First Week

Even a well-maintained MFP can generate support calls if users do not know how to use it correctly. Short, practical training can prevent many common problems.

Staff should know how to:

- Select the correct paper size.
- Print double-sided documents.
- Release secure jobs.
- Load paper without overfilling trays.
- Clear a simple paper jam safely.
- Scan to an approved destination.
- Choose colour or black-and-white output.
- Report faults with useful details.
- Avoid repeatedly resending a job when output is delayed.

Training should explain what staff must not do. Users should not pull paper forcefully, use incorrect media, open technical covers, or repeatedly restart a device displaying a serious error.

Create a simple reporting process with one email address, helpdesk form, or work-order portal. Every report should include the device location, error message, user department, and whether the problem affects printing, copying, scanning, or finishing.

A written maintenance manual should include routine procedures, equipment instructions, emergency processes, maintenance schedules, and staff responsibilities.  The same principle applies to MFP support: clear instructions reduce confusion and help technicians resolve problems faster.[^2]

## Match MFPs to School Workflows

MFP preparation is also an opportunity to review whether equipment is positioned and configured correctly.

Consider moving or reassigning devices if:

- A high-use MFP is far from the departments using it.
- Two buildings share one device and create long queues.
- A colour MFP is being used for routine black-and-white documents.
- A small desktop printer is handling volumes better suited to a shared MFP.
- Secure documents are being printed in an open public area.
- Teachers cannot access the device without assistance.

Schools should also review default settings. Double-sided printing can reduce paper use, while black-and-white defaults can prevent unnecessary colour costs. PIN release and mailbox printing can reduce abandoned output and improve document confidentiality.[^6]

The correct configuration depends on the school’s workflow. A finance office may need secure document handling, a library may need a simple copy function, and a central administration area may need high-volume scanning and finishing.

## Establish a Support and Maintenance Plan

Preparation should end with a written support plan, not just a completed checklist. The plan should state who handles first-level support, who contacts the service provider, and how urgent faults are escalated.

Review the service agreement for:

- Response time for urgent breakdowns.
- Availability of on-site technicians.
- Parts and labour coverage.
- Consumables included in the agreement.
- Remote monitoring arrangements.
- Loan equipment or backup options.
- Support hours during examinations and enrolment.
- Exclusions and additional charges.

Maintenance contracts should define the scope of services, emergency response times, performance measures, and procedures for adding or removing equipment as school needs change.[^3]

Schools with several buildings may benefit from a maintenance or asset-management system that records service history, assigns work orders, tracks spare parts, and prioritises critical devices.[^8]

## Final Back-to-School Checklist

Before the first day, confirm that:

- Every MFP is listed in the school’s asset inventory.
- Devices have been cleaned and professionally serviced where necessary.
- Printing, copying, scanning, and finishing functions have been tested.
- Toner, paper, staples, and replacement supplies are available.
- Network connections and print queues are working.
- Secure-print and user-authentication features have been checked.
- Scan destinations and permissions are correct.
- Staff know how to use the main functions.
- Fault-reporting procedures are visible and understood.
- Service contacts and escalation procedures are current.
- High-use devices are located where they are most needed.


## Conclusion

Educational institutions still depend on MFPs because schools manage a combination of digital and paper-based work. The devices support teaching, administration, finance, examinations, records management, and communication with families.

The best back-to-school preparation is practical and planned. Audit the fleet, service equipment before demand rises, test real workflows, stock essential supplies, secure confidential documents, train staff, and establish a clear support process. When these steps are completed before students return, MFPs become dependable tools instead of sources of disruption.
