# The Real Cost of Running an Aging Copier for One More Year: Why Some Industries Still Depend on It

Every office has one. The old copier in the corner that jams a little more often each month, hums a little louder, and takes a little longer to warm up. When budget season arrives, the easy decision is to keep it going for "just one more year." It still switches on, so why spend money replacing it?

The problem is that an aging copier rarely costs what it appears to cost. The purchase was paid for long ago, but the machine keeps charging you in quieter, less obvious ways, through repairs, wasted time, higher energy use, and growing security exposure. This article breaks down those real costs in plain terms, and explains why, despite all of this, some industries genuinely still lean on these machines every single day.

## The "one more year" trap

The logic behind keeping an old copier feels sound: it is already paid off, so anything it produces now is effectively free. In reality, the opposite is often true.

- **Reliability drops sharply near end of life.** Most office copiers and multifunction devices are designed to run well for roughly five to seven years. Past that point, wear on moving parts accelerates and breakdowns become more frequent rather than less.
- **The oldest year is usually the most expensive.** A machine's cheapest years are early in its life. The final year, when parts are failing and support is ending, is typically when it drains the most money and attention.
- **Free is an illusion.** "Free" only counts the original purchase. It ignores every rand, pound, or euro spent keeping the machine alive, and every hour your team loses when it stops.

Once you look past the sticker that was paid years ago, the running costs come into focus.

## Cost one: repairs, parts, and downtime

The most visible cost of an old copier is the growing bill to keep it working, but the deeper cost is the time your business loses while it is broken.

- **Parts become scarce.** As devices reach their official end of life, manufacturers stop making spare parts. Technicians are then forced to hunt for refurbished or salvaged components, which can turn a repair that once took hours into one that takes days.
- **Service visits multiply.** A machine that needed one visit a year may start needing several. Each call carries a cost, and each one is a signal that the equipment is past its dependable life.
- **Downtime stops work, not just printing.** When a shared copier fails, invoices go out late, contracts are not signed on time, and staff queue at a backup device or drive to a print shop. That lost productivity is often larger than the repair invoice itself.

A useful rule of thumb: when yearly repair and lost-productivity costs climb toward a significant share of what a replacement would cost, repairing is no longer the sensible choice.

## Cost two: energy and the environment

Older copiers are usually far less efficient than modern ones, and that inefficiency shows up on the electricity bill and in your organisation's environmental footprint.

- **Older machines draw more power.** Energy-efficiency standards have tightened considerably over the past decade. The European Union's [Ecodesign and energy-efficiency framework](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=legissum:200403_1) pushed newer office equipment toward lower consumption and smarter sleep modes that older units simply do not have.
- **E-waste is a growing global problem.** According to the [Global E-waste Monitor 2024](https://www.itu.int/en/ITU-D/Environment/Pages/Publications/The-Global-E-waste-Monitor-2024.aspx), published by the United Nations' training and research arm [UNITAR](https://unitar.org/about/news-stories/press/global-e-waste-monitor-2024-electronic-waste-rising-five-times-faster-documented-e-waste-recycling) and the International Telecommunication Union, the world generated a record 62 million tonnes of electronic waste in 2022, and it is rising five times faster than it is being recycled.
- **Disposal is a legal responsibility, not an afterthought.** In many regions, discarding office electronics is regulated. The EU's [Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment (WEEE) Directive](https://www.iea.org/policies/25162-waste-electrical-and-electronic-equipment-directive) places responsibility on how such equipment is collected and recycled, and industry bodies like [Waste Management World](https://waste-management-world.com/materials/the-global-e-waste-monitor/) continue to warn that discarded equipment is piling up faster than systems can handle.

Keeping an inefficient machine running is not the "greener" choice many assume. A well-managed replacement, responsibly recycled at the right time, is usually far better for both costs and the planet.

## Cost three: security and compliance

This is the cost most businesses overlook entirely, and it is often the most damaging. A modern copier is not a simple printing box. It is a networked computer with a built-in storage drive.

- **Copiers remember what they print.** Digital copiers and multifunction devices store images of the documents they scan, copy, and fax on an internal hard drive. Years of confidential material, contracts, medical forms, payroll records, can sit on that drive.
- **Old devices are soft targets.** The UK's [National Cyber Security Centre](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/device-security-guidance) notes that older networked devices frequently cannot support modern security controls, and its guidance on [using peripherals securely](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/device-security-guidance/policies-and-settings/using-peripherals-securely) treats them as a genuine part of the attack surface. A copier without current protection is a way into your network.
- **Disposal is where data leaks.** When an old copier is finally returned, resold, or scrapped without wiping its drive, sensitive data can walk straight out of the building. The UK [Information Commissioner's Office](https://ico.org.uk/media/1570/it_asset_disposal_for_organisations.pdf) is explicit that organisations remain responsible for personal data even after equipment leaves their premises, and its guidance on [deleting data from devices](https://ico.org.uk/for-the-public/online/deleting-your-data-from-computers-laptops-and-other-devices/) stresses that drives must be securely wiped or destroyed.

Under data-protection rules such as the GDPR, a leak from a poorly retired copier is not just embarrassing. It can bring regulatory penalties and lasting reputational harm.

## Cost four: the drag on everyday work

Beyond dramatic failures, an old copier quietly slows a business down every day.

- **Slow speeds add up.** A machine that takes longer to warm up and print wastes small pockets of time that, multiplied across a busy office, become significant.
- **Poor integration frustrates staff.** Older devices often will not connect cleanly to modern cloud tools, mobile printing, or secure scan-to-email workflows, forcing clumsy workarounds.
- **Quality suffers.** Faded prints and streaked scans mean re-runs, wasted paper and toner, and documents that reflect badly on your brand.

## Why some industries still depend on aging copiers

If the costs are so real, why do so many organisations hold on? Because for certain sectors, physical documents remain central to how work is legally and practically done. These are the industries where high-volume, reliable copying is not optional.

- **Legal.** Law firms handle enormous volumes of paperwork, and courts and clients still require physical files, signed originals, and certified copies. As specialists in [legal document handling](https://www.e-arc.co.uk/the-role-of-document-scanning-in-modern-legal-practices/) point out, the profession sits on some of the most document-intensive workflows of any sector, so a dependable device is mission-critical.
- **Healthcare.** Patient records, consent forms, and referrals often still move on paper, under strict confidentiality rules. Clinics need equipment that works every time and keeps sensitive information secure.
- **Government and public administration.** Public bodies manage vast archives of records, permits, tax documents, and minutes, much of it still paper-based and legally required to be retained and reproduced accurately.
- **Financial services.** Banks and insurers process large quantities of signed agreements, identity documents, and compliance paperwork that must be copied, archived, and produced on demand.
- **Education.** Schools and universities run continuous high-volume printing of exams, worksheets, and administrative forms, often on tight budgets that discourage replacement.

For these sectors, the copier is not a convenience. It is core infrastructure, which is exactly why running it into the ground is such a risk. When the machine that everything depends on fails, the disruption is far greater than in an office that prints occasionally.

## How to decide whether to keep or replace

You do not need to guess. A short, honest review usually makes the answer clear.

- **Add up the true annual cost.** Combine repairs, service calls, energy, and an estimate of downtime. Compare that total against the cost of a modern replacement over the same period.
- **Check the support status.** If the manufacturer has ended support and parts are hard to find, the machine is living on borrowed time.
- **Review the security position.** Ask whether the device can still be patched, whether its drive is encrypted, and whether you have a secure plan to wipe it at disposal.
- **Weigh the risk to critical work.** In a document-dependent industry, factor in what a multi-day outage would actually cost the organisation, not just the repair.
